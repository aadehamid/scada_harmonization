a combination of Mosquitto at the site edge + EMQX as the central UNS is more realistic and closer to how real plants preserve local autonomy and control.

Why combining both is realistic
Local autonomy and resilience. In real IIoT/industrial edge patterns, plants often run an on‑premise broker or gateway so they can keep exchanging OT data even if the WAN / central system is down. A lightweight broker like Mosquitto on a gateway or small server is exactly that.

Separation of concerns. The site broker focuses on local OT traffic, while the central broker (EMQX) focuses on enterprise‑wide UNS, cross‑site harmonization, and IT integrations.

Network and security boundaries. Often, sites expose only specific topics upstream, keeping a lot of detailed traffic inside the site network. A local broker plus a bridge lets you control what gets forwarded.

So your pattern:

Mosquitto = per‑site edge broker (local plant autonomy).

EMQX = central UNS broker (enterprise harmonization and fan‑out).

For your historian, TimescaleDB is the best fit for this lab, and it makes sense to treat it together with the Postgres decision (#7) rather than as a totally separate choice.

Below is why, relative to what you’re trying to learn and show.

How the three options stack up for your use case
InfluxDB OSS
Strengths: Purpose‑built time‑series DB, good integration with Grafana, and a familiar choice in IIoT circles.

Watch‑outs for you:

OSS lacks clustering and has a more complex product split (OSS vs cloud vs enterprise).

Flux / InfluxQL means you have a second query language to reason about, on top of SQL in the rest of the stack.

For a lab that already has Postgres, Neo4j, ERPNext, and Python in the mix, that extra cognitive surface might not actually teach you anything you can’t get from SQL.

QuestDB
Strengths: Very high ingest rates and SQL‑native querying, optimized for dense telemetry.

Watch‑outs:

Smaller ecosystem and fewer “industrial‑ready” examples and integrations compared with Influx/Timescale.

For your lab, the bottleneck will be modeling and integration, not pushing hundreds of thousands of rows per second, so its main advantage is less critical.

TimescaleDB (Postgres‑based)
Strengths:

It’s Postgres with time‑series extensions, so you get standard SQL plus hypertables, compression, retention, and time‑bucket tooling.

Ecosystem is huge (Grafana, BI tools, Python libraries) and looks just like Postgres from the outside.

Because it is Postgres, it can also act as the relational store for ODS/OLTP data if you decide to consolidate.

Watch‑outs:

Using one engine for both time‑series and relational does slightly blur the conceptual “boundary” between historian vs relational store, at least physically.

For your goals (education + realism + integration with ERPNext, Neo4j, floci, Python):

You get more integrative learning from one strong SQL‑based TSDB than from juggling a separate query language.

TimescaleDB lines up nicely with:

Python (psycopg, SQLAlchemy).

Grafana and other dashboards.

Potentially storing some ERP‑adjacent or ODS tables alongside telemetry.

Philosophical question: consolidate vs separate
You summarized it well:

Consolidate: Timescale for both historian and relational (Decision #3 + #7 in one engine).

Separate: Dedicated TSDB (Influx/QuestDB) + separate Postgres for relational.

Given the rest of your architecture:

You already have clear separations at OTHER layers:

Mosquitto vs EMQX vs Python forwarder.

ERPNext vs Neo4j vs UNS.

OT vs DMZ vs IT/cloud.

You also have to manage multiple engines anyway (Postgres for ERPNext, Neo4j for graph, etc.).

In that context, using TimescaleDB as “Postgres with time‑series” actually simplifies the infra without reducing the conceptual clarity:

You can still treat “historian schema” and “relational schema” as separate logical domains, even if they live in the same Postgres instance:

e.g., ts_historian.* schema for hypertables.

ods_core.* / erp_shadow.* for relational/ODS.

The educational boundary (time‑series vs relational modeling) is expressed at the schema and usage level, not only at the “different product” level.

So, for this lab, I’d recommend:

Decision #3: Historian → TimescaleDB.

Decision #7: Postgres deployment → Use TimescaleDB’s Postgres instance as both the historian (hypertables) and ODS/relational store, but keep schemas clearly separated in the design.

This gives you:

Realistic historian behavior (large time‑series tables, retention, downsampling).

Simple integration with Python and Grafana.

A clean story when you show how ERPNext/Neo4j/ML consume historian data via SQL.
